The shadow price of a joule

How a lap of telemetry becomes a number in seconds — identifying the car from its own data, solving the deployment problem with Pontryagin's maximum principle, and pricing every mistake against one quantity: what a joule is worth, right here, right now.

This is the reasoning behind the tool, written to be read start to finish. Every figure is drawn from real solver output on the test circuit — nothing here is illustrative.

00Why a race is not a qualifying lap

Every ERS guide you can find online solves the same problem: one lap, start full, finish empty, deploy on the exits. It is the easy case, and it is not the case you are in during a race.

Three things change, and all three are about time coupling — what you do now constrains what you can do later:

  • The lap has to repeat. Energy spent and not recovered is energy the next lap starts without. A quick lap bought with the battery is a loan, and it compounds across a stint.
  • Harvest is not a constant. With clear air ahead you brake later and recover less. The budget itself moves lap to lap, so the right answer moves with it.
  • Running dry has a location. Spending sector 1's energy leaves sector 3 empty, and how much that costs depends entirely on what sector 3 looks like.

None of this is answerable by a rule of thumb, because the answer depends on the circuit, the car, and the state of charge you happen to be in. It has to be recomputed. The rest of this document is how.

01Learning the car from its own telemetry

Before anything can be optimised, the car has to be modelled. Working in distance rather than time — the natural coordinate for a circuit — and per unit mass, longitudinal motion is:

d(v²/2)/ds = a·P(s)/v − drag(s)·v² − c drag(s) = b + db·aero(s) a powertrain efficiency ÷ mass b drag ÷ mass, high-downforce mode db drag change in straight-line mode (negative) c rolling and driveline losses ÷ mass aero(s) 0 = corner mode, 1 = straight-line mode

The pleasant surprise in the 2026 telemetry spec is that EnginePowerICE and EnginePowerMGUK are reported in watts. Deployment never has to be inferred from throttle position or state-of-charge deltas — it is measured directly, which is what makes every later number honest.

The equation is linear in (a, b, db, c), so this is a least-squares fit. Three things go wrong if you write that fit naively, and all three took real debugging.

The game reports speed as a whole number of km/h

One km/h is 0.278 m/s. Differencing that across a 10 m bin gives an acceleration estimate whose quantisation noise is larger than the signal — the fitted values came back with negative drag and a five-fold error in efficiency.

The fix is to stop differencing and start integrating. The same equation integrated over a longer baseline is still exactly linear in the same unknowns:

(e[i+W] − e[i]) / (W·ds) = a·⟨P/v⟩ − b·⟨v²⟩ − db·⟨v²·aero⟩ − c e = v²/2

With W chosen for a ~100 m baseline the quantisation averages away and the estimate stays unbiased. R² went from 0.30 to 0.99.

One lap cannot identify the parameters

A single lap has a single deployment pattern, which leaves power and drag nearly collinear. The fit then reproduces that lap beautifully and mispredicts every other one — useless here, because the entire job is predicting laps the driver has not driven yet.

The fix is classical: persistent excitation. Regression rows are pooled across laps, and different laps deploy differently, which is exactly the variation the fit needs to separate the two effects. Recovery against known truth went from a 5× error to 1.4%.

The car changes underneath you

Fuel burns off, tyres go away. The fit is ridge-regularised toward the previous estimate, in normalised column units so the penalty doesn't land entirely on whichever parameter happens to have the largest column. That makes it a recursive estimator: it tracks a changing car without lurching on one noisy lap.

02Power limited, or grip limited?

This step is small, easy to skip, and getting it wrong quietly corrupts everything downstream.

To know whether extra deployment would have made the car go faster at some point on track, you need to know what was limiting it there. The tempting test is throttle position: full throttle means the car wants more, so it must be power limited.

That test is wrong, and it is wrong in precisely the place this tool exists to talk about. On a corner exit the car is traction limited. It is at full throttle and already using every newton the tyres will accept. Extra power there does not produce extra speed — it produces wheelspin.

So the model compares, bin by bin, the acceleration actually achieved against what the fitted model says the delivered power should have produced. Where the car fell well short at full throttle, grip was the binding constraint, and the speed there stays capped at the driver's own best. Where it matched, power was binding, and the optimiser is free to find speed the driver never found.

Why this matters so much. Treating full throttle as power limited makes the optimiser promise corner-exit time that the tyres cannot deliver — it would recommend dumping energy into exits and then report a phantom gain when the driver complied. Fixing this took the model's lap-time error from 6.1% to 0.13%.

There is a second, subtler version of the same mistake. An early version capped speed at whatever the driver had achieved in each bin. On a lap where they never deployed on the exits, that "proved" deploying on the exits was impossible — the model would have quietly confirmed whatever the driver already did.

03The optimisation

Now the actual problem. Choose a deployment power u at every point on track to minimise lap time, without running the battery outside its limits, and finishing with as much charge as you started:

minimise T = Σ ds / v over u(s) ∈ [0, P_max(v)] subject to dE/ds = −u/v + h(s) 0 ≤ E ≤ E_cap E(L) ≥ E_target

The obvious approach is a two-dimensional dynamic program over distance and charge: discretise the battery into levels, and solve backwards. It works, and it is what I planned to build.

I built something better, and the reason is worth the detour.

Relaxing the constraint gives you a price

The energy budget is what makes this hard — it couples every point on track to every other point. So take it out of the constraints and put it into the objective, multiplied by a number λ:

min_u Σ [ dt + λ · u · dt ]

Now every point on track can be decided independently of the budget, because the budget has been replaced by a price. What remains is a one-dimensional dynamic program over speed alone — 56 speed levels × 9 power levels × 200 bins, milliseconds in numpy.

Then you tune λ until the solution happens to spend exactly the energy available. Spending falls monotonically as the price rises, so a plain bisection converges.

This is Pontryagin's maximum principle, and λ is the co-state. The reason it is worth preferring over the two-dimensional program is not that it is faster — though it is — but that λ is a real, interpretable quantity that falls out as a first-class object rather than as a finite difference of a value surface:

λ is the shadow price of energy: seconds per joule. It answers "if I had one more joule on this lap, how much lap time would it buy me?" On the test circuit, 0.50 seconds per megajoule.

The decision rule, and what a joule actually buys

With a price in hand the optimal policy becomes almost trivially interpretable. Define g(s) as the marginal seconds saved per joule deployed at a point. Then:

deploy where g(s) > λ the joule buys more than it costs coast where g(s) < λ it is worth more somewhere else

Corner exits beating the end of a straight is not a rule the tool was taught. It is what falls out, because g is large at low speed and collapses toward zero above the taper knee.

Computing g correctly is where I got it wrong the first time, and the bug is instructive. My first version measured the time saved inside that bin. But a joule spent on a corner exit does not just shorten that ten metres — the car leaves faster and stays faster all the way down the following straight, and almost all the benefit is in that tail. Measuring locally understated the value by a factor of twenty, which made the comparison against λ meaningless and silently broke the entire attribution layer.

The fix is the adjoint method: one backward pass that accumulates every downstream consequence, in O(n) rather than n separate re-simulations.

φ_i = ∂dt_i/∂v_i + ( ∂dt_i/∂v_next + φ_{i+1} ) · ∂v_next/∂v_i φ_i is the sensitivity of the whole remaining lap to speed at point i

Where the next speed is clamped — a braking zone, or a corner already taken at the limit — the derivative is zero and the chain terminates. Extra speed there is simply thrown away. That single term is why deploying into a braking zone or a traction-limited exit is worth nothing, and it is now a consequence of the maths rather than a special case in the code.

Checked against brute force. Perturbing deployment at a single point and re-simulating the entire lap agrees with the adjoint to four decimal places (0.7056 vs 0.7056 s/MJ; 0.6315 vs 0.6323 at a noisier point).

When the battery hits empty, the price jumps

The relaxation above assumes λ is a single number for the whole lap. That is true only while the charge stays strictly between its limits. The moment the battery hits zero mid-lap, energy before that point and energy after it are no longer interchangeable, and a single price cannot describe both.

The maximum principle says exactly what happens: the co-state is piecewise constant and jumps at the contact point. So the solver detects where charge would go negative, splits the lap there, and solves each segment with its own λ — targeting a small reserve at the join rather than exactly zero, because a plan that aims at precisely empty lands just below it, and a plan that schedules a negative battery is not one anyone can drive.

This is what makes "empty through sector 3" a case the solver handles rather than an error it reports.

The terminal condition, measured rather than guessed

One loose end. A lap is a loop, so the solver needs to know what speed at the finish line is worth — otherwise it coasts across the line to bank energy, or over-buys speed it cannot use.

I first set this to a hand-picked constant. It was more than double the true value, and the solver duly wasted 200 kW over the last 130 m of every lap. The honest version reads it off the adjoint: φ at the start line is the value of speed at the finish line, because they are the same place. The solver now recalibrates it from each solution, converging to 0.022 s per m/s against a brute-force measurement of 0.026.

04Turning a solved lap into one sentence

The optimiser produces a plan. The driver needs a diagnosis: one thing to fix, and what it is worth. This is where it is easiest to produce numbers that look authoritative and are quietly wrong, so the accounting is deliberately conservative.

Three quantities are kept strictly separate, because they are different currencies:

QuantityHow it is measured
Allocation loss
this lap		Simulated actual lap minus the optimal lap on exactly the energy you spent
Harvest loss
this lap		Re-solving on the energy your own best braking recovers in the same zones
Sustainability cost
next lap		λ × the charge deficit carried over the line

Two decisions that make the numbers trustworthy

Compare against the same energy you actually spent. This sounds like a detail and is the difference between working and not. If you compare a battery-dump lap against an energy-neutral optimum, the dump lap had more energy available, so its poor placement is masked by its larger budget and the whole thing nets out to nearly zero. Holding the energy fixed isolates placement; the overspending is then charged once, separately, to the next lap. Making this change moved a deliberately terrible lap from a reported 0.05 s to 0.93 s.

Let the economics rank, and the simulator total. The tempting move is to price each mistake as λ × the misplaced energy. On the first terrible lap that produced 2.02 s of "wasted deployment" against a lap whose entire measured loss was 0.66 s. The error is conceptual: λ is by definition the value of the last joule, and energy has diminishing returns, so pricing a large block at the marginal rate always overstates it. Now λ and g decide the ranking — which symptom dominates — and the total comes from the simulator, so the parts sum to something real.

06Does any of it work?

The honest test of a model that recommends counterfactuals is whether it can predict a lap it was not fitted to. So the synthetic session drives three laps one way and three laps another, and the model — fitted on all of them — is asked to reproduce each.

The structural checks matter as much as the numbers: lap time is monotone in the energy budget, λ falls as energy becomes less scarce, charge never leaves its bounds, and qualifying mode beats energy-neutral mode. Any of those failing would mean the solver was not solving the stated problem.

All of this is on synthetic telemetry where the truth is known by construction. It has not yet met the real game. The packet layouts are verified byte-exact against the 2026 spec and the parser self-checks, but the first live session is where anything I could not simulate will show up — record one with --record and the fidelity number will tell you honestly.

09What I would do next

  • Model the car ahead explicitly. Traffic currently enters only through the measured harvest and the speed envelope. delta_front is already parsed; using it to predict the braking you are about to be forced into would let the solver plan for traffic rather than react to it.
  • Give the override its own taper. The 2026 manual override shares the learned deployment curve; it deserves a separate one.
  • Optimise across laps, not within one. The energy-neutral constraint is a sensible proxy for stint-level planning, but the real problem is a multi-lap one where you deliberately run down the battery to defend a position and rebuild it later.
  • Tyre and fuel state. The recursive fit tracks these implicitly as drift; making them explicit states would let the model anticipate rather than follow.
